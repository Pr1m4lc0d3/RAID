# raid-stunt

Never fight where the money wins. A budget buys reach directly; a stunt buys reach by getting someone else to carry it for free. If nobody carries it, you didn't run a stunt — you spent effort on a stand-in for an ad, without the ad's reach.

## 1. The carry test

Run the floor in section 2 first. It is written second because it is shorter, not because it is checked later — a play that fails the floor is not rescued by passing anything here, and the later you look at it, the more work you have already sunk into the play you now have to bin.

Before building anything, ask: **if this works, who repeats it, and why is repeating it in their interest?**

Name the person. Not "people will share this" — which person, in which room, gets something out of repeating it? A discount for referring, a laugh worth passing on, a genuinely useful tool they'd recommend anyway, proof of a claim they wanted proof of. If you can't name the person and the reason, the honest answer is nobody, and the honest label for what you're about to build is a chore, not a stunt.

The strongest stunts pass this test structurally: **only you can run them**, because the doing of the stunt *is* a demonstration of the thing you sell. A speed claim proven live against the clock, or against your own previous version, or against the published standard the category is measured by. A durability claim proven by handing the product to strangers to abuse. A "this takes all week" claim proven by doing the whole thing in front of people, unedited, in the time you said it takes. If a competitor copies a stunt like that, the copy still points at you — they're now demonstrating your category, on your terms.

**Demonstrate against yourself, a published standard, or the problem — never against a named rival.** A play built on beating a named competitor in public is a comparative attack, and `raid-asymmetry`'s naming rule forbids that anywhere a stranger can read: the asymmetry is internal ammunition, not the stunt. It is also how a demonstration becomes a disparagement claim, because a contest you designed is a benchmark you chose, and they get to argue in front of the same audience that you chose it to make them lose. Beat your own last version instead — nobody can dispute your standing to run that comparison, and the result is the same proof.

## 2. The floor

Some plays are not raids at all, whatever they would achieve. Reject these before the carry test is worth running, because the carry test does not catch them — a hoax passes it brilliantly:

- **Nothing unlawful.** No criminal damage, no flyposting or stickering property you do not own, no trespass, no obstruction. "It's only a sticker" is a sentence people say to police.
- **No hoaxes and no impersonation.** Not of a person, not of a brand, not of an authority. No fake announcement, no fake account, no fabricated endorsement, no staged event presented as real. **A play that works because people believe something false is a lie with a marketing budget**, and it is the fastest way to destroy the standing you are trying to build — the reveal is not a punchline, it is the moment the audience learns you will deceive them for reach.
- **Nothing that could read as a threat, an emergency, or a real incident.** No unattended packages, no staged distress, no alarm, nothing that could put a stranger or an emergency service on the wrong footing. Public alarm is not attention.
- **No breach of a platform's terms.** Automation the terms forbid, engagement the terms forbid, an account used the way the terms forbid. A stunt that gets the account removed took ground you cannot hold.

Underneath all four: **disproportion is not a licence.** The gate asks whether a play is disproportionate. It does not ask whether it is permitted — that is a separate question with its own answer, and a play has to pass both. A play that clears the carry test and fails this floor is not a good raid with a problem attached; it is not a raid.

## 3. Failure modes

Reject a stunt design if it matches any of these:

- **It insults the audience it needs.** A stunt that makes fun of the people you're trying to reach doesn't get carried — it gets reported. The line between "cheeky" and "insulting" is drawn by the room's own norms (see `raid-recon`), not by whether it made your team laugh.
- **It would embarrass you if it worked.** If going viral is the failure case — because the thing you'd be known for isn't the thing you're selling — don't build it on a bet that it flops quietly instead.
- **It requires a budget to be noticed.** Paid boosting, paid seeding, paying for placement to make the stunt look organic — that's an ad wearing a stunt's clothes, and money wins there. If the plan only works with a media buy behind it, it isn't a stunt.

## 4. Claim discipline still binds a stunt

The loudest play is the one most likely to be checked, screenshotted, and quoted back. A stunt built on a number nobody verified, or a comparison nobody sourced, doesn't just risk being wrong — it risks being wrong in front of the largest audience the play will ever get. Where `FORTRESS` is installed, `fortress-truth`'s register binds every claim inside a stunt exactly as it binds ordinary copy; there is no exemption for something that worked. Where it isn't installed, source it anyway — the exposure is the same either way.

## 5. What this doesn't decide

`raid-stunt` designs the play, holds it to the floor, and filters it through the carry test. Whether now is the right moment to run it is `raid-moment`. Whether a claim inside it is sourced is `fortress-truth` where present, and plain honesty where it isn't.

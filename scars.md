# Scars — what this plugin's doctrine was learned from

This is RAID's own incident log, not an adopter's. It documents what the doctrine in this plugin
cost to learn, before it was doctrine. Each entry is written generically — no product, company,
or domain is named — because the lesson has to survive leaving this specific incident behind.
**The lesson generalizes even though the incident does not.**

This file is separate from the adopter's own `.monkeys/scars.md`, generated empty at kickoff and
filled in as the adopter's own incidents happen. Don't confuse the two.

---

## 1. A channel that looks like revenue can run backward

**Incident.** A category of publishing venues was evaluated as a paid distribution channel on the
strength of a widespread belief that they pay contributors for written pieces. Every venue actually
checked, once the submission terms were read instead of assumed, charged the submitter instead — for
placement, review, or a named "programme" fee. The payment ran from the party trying to reach an
audience to the platform, not the other way around.

**Damage.** A planned revenue path was built and pursued on the inverted premise before the
direction of payment was checked. The time spent researching the category and preparing submissions
for it produced nothing, because the channel was never going to pay in the direction assumed.

**Rule.** Before treating any channel as income, verify which way the money actually moves — read
the venue's own submission or pricing terms, not the reputation the category has. A channel that is
widely believed to pay is not the same claim as a channel that has been checked and found to pay.

## 2. An exclusion without a written reason gets re-proposed forever

**Incident.** A channel had been ruled out early, for reasons that were sound at the time — a rules
violation, a bad fit for the audience, a cost that didn't pencil out. The decision itself was never
written down anywhere durable; it lived only in the memory of whoever made it. In session after
session that followed, the same channel came back up as a fresh idea, was re-evaluated from
scratch, and was ruled out again each time, at the cost of re-deriving the same conclusion
repeatedly.

**Damage.** Recurring effort spent re-litigating a decision that had already been made correctly
once — time paid twice, and in places three or more times, for a single answer.

**Rule.** An exclusion without a written reason is not a decision; it's a recurring argument that
hasn't found its file yet. Record why a channel was ruled out, in the same place the channel would
have been listed if it had been kept — that record is what stops the loop, not the memory of the
person who made the call.

## 3. A metric picks the wrong asset when judgement was needed

**Incident.** A creative asset — one of several candidate versions of the same piece — was selected
using a measurable proxy: a score, a click-through rate, an engagement number from a quick test.
The proxy favored one version clearly, and that version shipped on the strength of the number
alone. The person who had to stand behind the asset day to day — represent it, answer for it, live
with how it read — had not been the one to judge it; the number had judged it for them. The choice
was reversed after the fact, once the actual owner looked at it directly, and the work was redone.

**Damage.** The selected asset was rejected after shipping, and the work of producing and placing
it had to be repeated with a version chosen by direct judgement instead.

**Rule.** A proxy metric tells you how something measures. It never tells you whether it's right,
and it cannot make a judgement call on behalf of the person who has to own the outcome. Use a
metric to narrow candidates, never to make the final call in place of the person who has to live
with it.
